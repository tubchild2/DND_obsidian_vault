### Theories of Self-Development
##### Psychological Theories
**Sigmund Freud**
- Divided the maturation process into stages
- Posited that people's self-development is closely linked to their early stages of development
- According to Freud, failure to engage in or disengage from a specific stage results in emotional and psychological consequences throughout adulthood

**Erik Erikson (lazy worldbuilding)**
- Believed personality continued to change over time and was never truly finished
- Erikson's view gave credit to more social aspects, instead of Freud's psychosexual stages. 

**Jean Piaget**
- Focused on the role of social interactions in child development.
- The development of self evolved through a negotiation between the world that exists in one's mind and the world that exists as it is experienced socially. 

**Harry Harlow**
- Studied the role of social relationships in human development
- Observed the impact of isolation and maternal deprivation on the development of young rhesus monkeys
- Isolated monkeys self-harmed, and had a difficult time integrating when reconnected with other monkeys
- The monkeys developed attachment to simulated mothers made of cloth and wire

**Psychology vs. Sociology**
- Both study people and their behavior
- Psychologists focus on how the mind influences behavior
- Sociologists study the role of society in shaping behavior

##### Sociological Theories
**Charles Cooley**
- People's self understanding is constructed by their perception of how others view them

**George Herbert Mead**
- Studied the self. We aren't born with the ability to view ourselves through the eyes of others. This assists us with becoming self aware.
- Mead believed we become ourselves through socialization. 
	- Children can only imitate; they can't imagine how others see things
	- Play stage. Children can take on the role that one other person might have. They may imitate a parent's role by playing dress up. 
	- Game stage. Children can consider several roles at the same time and how those roles interact with one another. They understand how different roles at a restaurant contribute to the function of the business. 
	- Finally, the stage where they learn the idea of the generalized other. They know what to expect of society, and can see the self. 

**The Self**
- A person's distinct identity that is developed through social interaction.

**The Generalized Other**
- The common behavioral expectations of general society. 


**Kohlberg**
- Studied moral development
- Preconventional
	- Young children, who lack higher cognitive ability, experience the world around them only through their senses.
- Conventional
	- Teenagers become increasingly aware of others' feelings and take those into consideration when determining what's "good" and "bad."
- Postconventional
	- When people begin to think of morality in abstract terms. 
	- People recognize that legality and morality do not always match up evenly. 

**Moral Development**
- The way people learn what society considers to be "good" and "bad." 


**Carol Gilligan**
- Kohlberg only used male subjects, so she wanted to test women. 
- Boys appeared to have a justice perspective, by placing emphasis on rules and laws. 
- Girls seemed to have a care and responsibility perspective; they consider people's reasons behind behavior that seems morally wrong. 
- It's been mostly discredited due to its small sample size, and the results haven't been replicated. 


**Lisa Bloom**
- Found that 50% of girls ages 3-6 worry about being fat. 
- Possibly because of gender-oriented pleasantries related to girl's appearances.


**Egalia preschool in Sweden**
- Children are developing in a genderless environment.
- Everyone is referred to with neutral terms
- Absolutely no reinforcement of gender expectations takes place. 
- Maybe a tad extreme and harmful when they get out into society. 


### Why Socialization Matters
Socialization is the means in which culture travels and is perpetuated. 
Socialization provides the means via which we become able to see ourselves through the eyes of others.
To function in society, we need to understand culture, which requires language, which requires socialization. 

**Nature vs Nurture**
	Megamind reference? Are heroes born or made? 
	The answer is both/it depends, but that's lame. 
	**Nature**
		The argument that who we are is a product of genetics. Are people naturally inclined to a certain kind of behavior from early on? Identical twins that were separated from birth still behave somewhat similarly. 
	**Nurture**
		The argument that who we are is a result of society and the relationships that surround us.

**Functionalist Perspective**
	Socialization is important for healthy individual and societal development. 
	It's essential to society because it trains members to operate successfully within it and because it perpetuates culture by transmitting it to new generations. 

**Conflict Theorist Perspective**
	Socialization reproduces inequality from generation to generation by conveying different expectations and norms to those with different social characteristics. 

**Interactionist Perspective**
	Obviously they're concerned with face-to-face exchanges and symbolic communication. 


### Agents of Socialization
**Social Group Agents**
	Families, peer groups, etc.
	Provide the first experiences of socialization

**Families**
	The first agent of socialization
	Teach them how to use objects, how to relate to others, and how the world works. 
	Many social factors affect the way a family raises its children.  
	Children are taught to obey behaviors beneficial to their parents, perpetuating the class system

**Peer Groups**
	Made up of people who are similar in age and social status and who share interests.  
	Begins in the earliest years.

**Institutional Agents**
	Social institutions of a culture. Schools, workplaces, and the government. 

**Schools**
	Schools serve a latent function in society by socializing children into behaviors like practicing teamwork, following a schedule, and using textbooks. 
	Sociologists call this aspect of schools the **hidden curriculum**

**Workplace**
	Workers require new socialization into a workplace, in terms of both material culture and nonmaterial culture. 
	In recent years, it's become more common to take a new job every decade or so, socializing people to many more working environments. 

**Religions**
	These places teach participants how to interact with the religion's material culture and nonmaterial culture. 

**Governments**
	Many rites of passage come from age norms established by the government. Like being defined as an adult or being allowed to drink or have sex. 

**Mass Media**
	Mass media distribute impersonal information to a wide audience via tv, newspapers, radio, and the internet. 
	Media greatly influences social norms, since people spend multiple hours a day engaging with it. 
	They learn about material culture (things like ads) and nonmaterial culture (things like family values (not the show))


### Socialization Across the Life Course
Socialization is a lifelong process.

**Resocialization**
	In the process, old behaviors that were helpful in a previous role are removed because they are no longer of use. 
	There are many cases where the old rules don't apply: moving to a senior care center, going to a boarding school, being on a ship at sea, or serving a sentence in the prison system. 
	Many people consider training or retraining processes to embody elements of resocialization. 

**Degradation Ceremony**
	Members entering a new institution must abandon their old identity. 
	New members lose the aspects of their old identity and are given new identities. 
	This process can be gentle or harsh.
