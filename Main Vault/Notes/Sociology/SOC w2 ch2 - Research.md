##### Scientific Method
**Overview**
Provides a systematic and organized series of steps that help ensure objectivity and consistency in exploring a social problem. They provide means for accuracy, reliability, and validity.  

**Question** 
Ask a question, select a problem, and identify the specific area of interest. It should be narrow enough to study within a geographic location and time frame. It should also be broad enough to have universal merit. 

**Research**
Conduct thorough background research through a literature review. Visiting the library, doing online research, and surveying academic journals will uncover existing research about the topic of study. 
This step is important to make it so that their work will be building on something new rather than being completely isolated, and it also fills in any gaps in their understanding. 

**Hypothesis**
A hypothesis is an explanation for a phenomenon based on a conjecture about the relationship between the phenomenon and one or more causal factors. A hypothesis might form an if then statement. Usually, the hypothesis would relate to how one form of social behavior impacts another. 

**Study**
Researchers design studies to maximize reliability by using accurate methods. They also strive for validity with by developing an operational definition. 

**Conclusions**
After constructing the research design, sociologists collect, tabulate / categorize, and analyze the data to formulate conclusions. 

**Report**
Researchers then report their results at conferences and in academic journals. They're then subjected to the scrutiny of other sociologists in the field. It's important to peer review!


##### Research Methods
**Surveys**
Collect data from subjects who respond to a series of questions about behaviors and opinions. The way people act and they way they say they act are two very different things though. They may provide people with questionnaires, or use random polling. In a random poll. everyone from the selected population has an equal chance of being selected, preventing any potential biases in the produced survey results. 
Questionnaires come in multiple forms: they can be a series on closed ended questions, like yes or no or multiple choice. This is quantitative data that can be mathematically analyzed. They can also ask open ended questions which may result in qualitative data that must be more thoroughly dissected, but they're much more in-depth. 
Interviews are one-on-one conversations between the researcher and the subject. They aren't really limited in their responses in the way questionnaires are. Gaining the subject's trust can improve results. 

**Field Research**
Field research refers to gathering primary data from a natural environment. Sociologists step into new environments and observe, participate, or experience those worlds. This makes the sociologist the one out of their element. The purpose is to observe specific behaviors in the given field. Can't differentiate cause and effect, but can identify correlations.   

**Participant Observation**
A study in which researchers join people and participate in a group's routine activities for the purpose of observing them within that context. They might not compromise their identity or purpose if they feel it would compromise the results of their work. They simply want to observe and learn. 

**Ethnography**
The immersion of the researcher in the natural setting of an entire social community to observe and experience their everyday life and culture. An ethnographer would commit to spending a determined amount of time studying every aspect of the chosen place.
Institutional ethnography focuses intentionally on everyday concrete social relationships. Considered a feminist inspired approach to social analysis. Historically, social science research tended to objectify and ignore women and their experiences. 

**Case Study**
An in-depth analysis of a single event, situation, or individual. Examines existing sources, conducts interviews, engages in direct observation and even participant observation if possible. It provides extreme depth, but lacks in evidence to form a generalized conclusion. Useful when a single case is unique. 

**Experiments**
One way researchers test social theories is by conducting an experiment, meaning they investigate relationships to test a hypothesis. Lab based experiments and natural/field experiments. Useful for testing if-then theories. 
- In a lab setting, the research can be controlled so that more data can be recorded in a limited amount of time. 
- In a natural environment, the time it takes to gather data cannot be controlled by the information might be more accurate. 

**Secondary Data Analysis**
Does not result from firsthand research collected from primary sources, but are the already completed work of other researchers or data collected by an agency or organization. This saves time and money and can add depth. Public records are not always easy to access, though. I'd personally use CRAPP for this. 


##### Ethical Concerns
Sociology comes with an enormous amount of responsibility. They must avoid harming human subjects or groups while conducting research. Max Weber thought that personal values could distort the framework for disclosing study results. Sociologists must specifically value neutrality. 

**AMERICAN SOCIOLOGICAL ASSOCIATION**
The ASA is the major professional organization of sociologists in North America. They maintain a code of ethics consisting of principles and ethical standards to be used in the discipline. These helps researchers to do the following:
1. Maintain objectivity and integrity
2. Respect subjects' rights to privacy and dignity
3. Protect subjects from personal harm
4. Preserve confidentiality
5. Seek informed consent
6. Acknowledge collaboration and assistance
7. Disclose sources of financial support


##### Key Points
**Literature Review**
A review of any existing similar / related studies.

**Independent Variable (IV)**
Cause of the change that is being studied.

**Dependent Variable (DV)**
The effect / change that is being studied. 

**Reliability**
How likely research results are to be replicated if the study is reproduced.

**Accuracy**
Accurate tools and methods increase reliability. 

**Validity**
How well the study measures what it was designed to measure. 

**Operational Definition**
Sociologists develop operational definition by defining each concept, or variable, in terms of physical or concrete steps it takes to objectively measure it. 

**Interpretive Framework**
A systematic approach that doesn't follow the hypothesis-testing model that seeks to find generalizable results.. Instead, it seeks to understand social worlds from the point of view of participants, which leads to an in-depth knowledge or understanding about the human experience. It's more descriptive and narrative in its findings. 

**Critical Sociology / The Frankfurt School**
Focuses on deconstruction of existing sociological research and theory. They propose that social science is embedded in the system of power constituted by the set class, caste, race, gender, and other relationships that exist in society, so it can't be treated as purely objective. 

**Primary Source Data Collection**
- Survey
- Participant Observation
- Ethnography
- Case Study
- Unobtrusive Observation
- Experiment

**Secondary Data Analysis**
- Use of existing sources

**Experimental Group**
Exposed to the independent variable

**Control Group**
Not exposed to the independent variable

**Hawthorne Effect**
People change how they behave when they know they're being observed and studied. 

**Nonreactive Research**
Research that does not involve direct contact with subjects and will not alter or influence people's behaviors. 

**Content Analysis**
A systematic approach to record and value information gleaned from secondary data as they relate to the study at hand.
