## Racial, Ethnic, and Minority Groups
##### Race
**Race**
	Superficial physical differences that a particular society considers significant
	A grouping of humankind based on shared physical or social qualities

**Johann Friedrich Blumenbach**
	Introduced one of the famous groupings by studying human skulls. Largely rejected today. 
	Insulting to refer to Asian or Native American people this way, but largely acceptable to refer to White or Black people this way. 
	- Caucasian or White. People of European, Middle Eastern, and North African origin. 
	- Ethiopian or Black. People of sub-Saharan Africans origin
	- Malayan or Brown. People of Southeast Asian origin and Pacific Islanders
	- Mongolian or Yellow. People of all East Asian and some Central Asian origin.
	- American or Red. People of North American origin or American Indians. 

**Social Construction of Race**
	More accepted way of understanding racial categories. 

**The Bell Curve (1994)**
	Refutes the idea that biological-racial bases impact intelligence. 
	Concluded that lower IQ among a racial group was a product of nurture, not nature. 


##### Ethnicity
**Ethnicity**
	A shared culture--the practices, norms, values, and beliefs of a group that might include language, religion, and traditions, among other commonalities. 

**National Origin**
	It has to do with the geographic and political associations with a person's birthplace or residence. 

**Nationality**
	Legal status of belonging to a nation


##### Minority Groups
**Minority Groups**
	Any group of people who, because of physical or cultural traits, are singled out from others in society for differential and unequal treatment. 
	Connotes discrimination.
	In the US, the elderly might be a minority group due to diminished status as a product of discrimination. 

**Denote vs Connote**
	Denote means to represent the literal meaning of something. 
	Connote means to imply the nonmaterial figurative meaning of something

**Subordinate Group**
	Interchangeable with minority group.

**Dominant Group**
	Group that represents rulers or is in the majority who can access power and privilege in a given society. 

**Apartheid**
	In South Africa, and a minority group despite being the numerical majority. 

**Charles Wagley and Marvin Harris**
	The five characteristics of a minority group:
	1. Unequal treatment and less power over their lives
	2. Distinguishing physical or cultural traits like skin color or language
	3. Involuntary membership in the group
	4. Awareness of subordination
	5. High rate of in-group marriage

**Scapegoat Theory**
	Developed from Dollard's 1939 Frustration-Aggression theory. 
	Suggests the dominant group will displace its unfocused aggression onto a subordinate group.
	Like Hitler with the Jews. 

**Exogamy**
	Marriage outside a person's core social unit. 


## Perspectives
**Functionalist Perspective**
All of the elements of society have functions that promote solidarity and maintain order. 
Problems arise when one or more racial or ethnic groups experience inequalities.
This creates tension resulting in temporary dysfunction. It's expensive, a waste, and causes disorder. 
Racism can contribute positively to the functioning of society by strengthening bonds between in-group members through the ostracism of out-group members. 
In the view of functionalism, they must have served some important function to exist this long. 

**Conflict Theorist Perspective**
Conflict theories often apply to gender, class, education, race, and ethnicity based inequalities.

**Jim Crow Laws**
Severely limited black political and social power after the Civil War. 

**Vivien Thomas**
Black surgical technician who developed the technique that saved the lives of "blue babies" who was classified as a janitor for many years, and paid as such, despite the fact that he conducted complicated surgical experiments. 

**Interactionist Perspective**
Race and ethnicity provide strong symbols as sources of identity. 

**Herbert Blumer**
Proposes that symbols of race, not race itself, lead to racism. 
Someone who doesn't associated with the subordinate group, and who's only exposure to them is through other members of the dominant group / media, develop an abstract picture of the subordinate group and develop racist beliefs. 

**Intersection Theory**
Suggests that we can't separate the effects of race, class, gender, sexual orientation, etc.
Multiple layers intersect to change how we experience race. 

**Culture of Prejudice**
The theory that prejudice is embedded in our culture. 
We grow up surrounded by images of stereotypes and casual expressions of racism and prejudice. 


## Prejudice, Discrimination, and Racism
**Stereotypes**
	Oversimplified generalizations about groups of people. 
	Can be based on race, ethnicity, age, gender, sexual orientation, etc. Pretty much anything. 
	They may be positive, but are often negative.
	New stereotypes are rarely created, but are usually recycled from subordinate groups that have assimilated into society are are reused to describe newly subordinate groups. 

**Prejudice**
	The beliefs, thoughts, feelings, and attitudes someone holds about a group. 
	Not based on personal experience; it's a prejudgment, originating outside of experience. 
	Experience can lead people to feeling that their prejudice is confirmed or justified. 
	It's a type of confirmation bias. 
	Most often taught and learned.

**Discrimination**
	Unlike prejudice, which is biased thinking, discrimination is biased actions against a group of people. 

**Institutional Discrimination**
	Occurs when a societal system has developed with embedded disenfranchisement of a group. 

**White Privilege**
	The societal privilege that benefits white people, or those perceived to be white.
	Most white people don't want to acknowledge it because it lessens their accomplishments. 
	It does a little bit, but it's an institutional thing, not a personal thing. 

**Racism**
	Stronger type of prejudice and discrimination used to justify inequalities against individuals by maintaining that one racial category is somehow superior to inferior to others. 
	Used by the racial dominant group to maximize advantages for itself by disadvantaging racial minority groups. 

**Individual or Interpersonal Racism**
	Prejudice and discrimination executed by individuals consciously and unconsciously that occurs between individuals. Like telling a racist joke and believing in the superiority of White people. 

**Systemic Racism**
	Also called structural racism or institutional racism. Systems and structures that have procedures or processes that disadvantages racial minority groups. 
	Causes implicit bias in members. 

**Racial Profiling**
	Type of systemic racism that involves singling out racial minorities for differential treatment, usually harsher treatment. 

**Historical Racism**
	Economic inequality or social disparity caused by past racism. 

**Cultural Racism**
	Occurs when the assumption of of inferiority of one or more races is built into the culture of a society. 

**Colorism**
	Form of racism, based on the belief one type of skin tone is superior or inferior to another within a racial group. 

**Color-Avoidance Racism**
	An avoidance of racial language by European Americans that ignores the fact that racism continues to be an issue. 

**Racial Steering**
	In which real estate agents direct prospective homeowners toward or away from certain neighborhoods based on their race. 

**Racist Attitudes and Beliefs**
	Harder to pin down than racist practices. 
	Prejudice is harder to spot than discrimination. 

**Implicit Bias**
	Also called unconscious bias. 
	The process of associating stereotypes or attitudes towards categories of people without conscious awareness. 
	Causes structural racism. 

**Sedimentation of Racial Inequality**
	Describes the intergenerational impact of both practical and legalized racism that limits the abilities of Black people to accumulate wealth. 

**Redlining**
	Routinely refusing mortgages for households and businesses located in predominately minority communities. 

#### How to Be an Antiracist
**Antiracism**
	A radical choice in the face of history, requiring a radical reorientation of our consciousness. 
	We must work collaboratively within ourselves, our institutions, and our networks to challenge racism at local, national, and global levels. 

**Step 1**
	Understand and own the racist ideas in which we have been socialized and the racist biases that these ideas have created within each of us. 

**Step 2**
	Identity racist policies, practices, and procedures, and replace them with antiracist policies, practices, and procedures.



## Intergroup Relationships
**Pluralism**
	Mutual respect on the part of all cultures, both dominant and subordinate, creating a multicultural environment of acceptance. 
	Antithetical to assimilation. 

**Assimilation**
	Describes the process by which a minority individual or group gives up its own identity by taking on the characteristics of the dominant culture. 
	Some groups only keep symbolic gestures of their original ethnicity. Like Irish Americans celebrating St. Patrick's Day, or Hindu Americans celebrating Diwali. 
	Measured with four benchmarks
		1. Socioeconomic status
		2. Spatial concentration
		3. Language assimilation
		4. Intermarriage

**Amalgamation**
	The process by which a minority group and a majority group combine to form a new group. 
	Creates the melting pot analogy, unlike Pluralism's salad bowl analogy. 

**Miscegenation**
	Interracial Marriage. Extremely rare in the united states prior to the 20th century
	Sexual subordination of enslaved people resulted in children of mixed race, they were usually considered Black, and therefore, property. 

**Antimiscegenation**
	The banning of intermarriage between races. 
	Existed in the US until 1967's Loving v. Virginia

**Genocide**
	The deliberate annihilation of a targeted, usually subordinate, group. 

**Holocaust**
	The most famous genocide. The explicit goal of Hitler's Final Solution was the eradication of European Jewish people, as well as Catholics, the disabled, LGBTQ people, and Romani people.
	He also attempted to wipe out Jehovah's Witnesses. They could sign away their faith and leave, but they refused to break. They wore purple triangles. ~6000 were sent to prisons and camps, and ~1700 died from execution, mistreatment, or refusal to compromise their beliefs. Very few gave in. I read the Holocaust Encyclopedia page on Jehovah's Witnesses and it made me tear up. 

**Expulsion**
	Refers to a subordinate group being forced to leave a certain area or country. 
	In the 40s, after Pearl Harbor, anyone with as little as one-eighth Japanese ancestry could be put in internment camps for up to 4 years. In the 1990s, the US issued an apology and reparation efforts continue today. 

**Segregation**
	The physical separation of two groups.

**De Jure Segregation**
	Segregation enforced by law
	Apartheid stripped Black South Africans of their civil rights and forcibly relocated them. 
	Occurred in the US for many years after the Civil War, especially in former Confederate states. 

**Brown v. Board of Education**
	In 1954, the Supreme Court declared that separate educational facilities were inherently unequal. 
	Few institutions desegregated as a result of it. Government and even military intervention was required. 

**De Facto Segregation**
	Segregation that occurs without laws but because of other factors
	Still exists in the US. Different racial or ethnic groups are segregated by neighborhood, borough, or parish.

#### Native Americans
**Native Americans**
	Indigenous peoples
	The only nonimmigrant people in the United States
	Includes American Indians, Alaska Natives, Native Hawaiians, Chamorros, American Samoans, Canadian First Nations natives, and natives of indigenous communities in Mexico and Central and South America. 
	Once numbered in the millions, but by 2010 made up only 0.9% of the U.S. populace. 

**Pre-Columbian culture**
	Native American culture prior to European settlement. 

**Intergroup Relations**
	Europeans and Native American relations were brutal. Genocide, intentional and by germs, wiped out almost all of the indigenous population. 
	The French, English, and Dutch took what land they wanted and expanded across the continent at will. 

**Indian Removal Act of 1830**
	Forced the relocation of any Native tribes east of the Mississippi River to lands west of the river

**Indian Appropriation Acts**
	Funded further removals and declared that no Indian tribe could be recognized as an independent nation, tribe, or power with which the US government would have to make treaties. 

**The Dawes Act of 1887**
	Reversed the policy of isolating Native Americans on reservations, instead forcing them onto individual properties that were intermingled with White settlers, thereby reducing their capacity for power as a group. 

**Off Reservation Boarding Schools**
	Run by Christian missionaries and the US government to "civilize" Native American children and assimilate them into White society. Located away from the children's families and culture. 
	Children were forced to cut their hair, speak English, and practice Christianity. They were physically and sexually abused for decades. Only in 1987 did the Bureau of Indian Affairs issue a policy on sexual abuse in boarding schools. 

**Current Status**
	The eradication of Native American culture continued until the 1960s, when Native Americans were able to participate in and benefit from the civil rights movement. 
	**Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968**
		Guaranteed Indian tribes most of the rights of the United States Bill of Rights. 
	**Indian Self-Determination Act of 1975**
		Recognized tribal governments and gave them more power. 


#### African Americans
**Misnomers**
	Can refer to many people with dark skin with roots in Europe or the Caribbean. 
	Actual immigrants from Africa may feel that they have more of a claim to the term African American than those who are many generations removed from ancestors who originally came to this country. 

**How and Why They Came**
	Did not come here by choice. They were originally brought as indentured servants, and worked side by side with White servants for a century. That was until the Slave Codes of 1705, which declared that any foreign-born non-Christian could be enslaved, and that enslaved people were property. 

**US Slavery**
	Persisted for 150 years. Black Africans were kidnapped from their own lands and shipped to the New World on the trans-Atlantic journey known as the Middle Passage. 
	In colonial and later US slave codes made it so that any children of enslaved people were also enslaved people. So the slave class was created. 
	By 1808, enslaved people were bought and sold across state lines like livestock. 

**Intergroup Relations**
	Slaveholders and their supporters viewed Black people as innately inferior. 
	They were denied even the most basic rights of citizenship. 
	Conflict theorists say the dominant group needed complete control over the subordinate group in order to maintain its power. 
	Whippings, executions, rapes, and denial of schooling and health care were widely practiced. 

**Civil Rights Act of 1964**
	Banned discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin. 

**Current Status**
	True equality doesn't yet exist, but it's generally improving. 


#### Asian Americans
**Chinese Immigration**
	The Chinese immigrated in the mid-nineteenth century. 
	Primarily men whose intention was to work for several years in order to earn incomes to support their families in China. 
	Primary destination was the American West, where the Gold Rush was
	They helped construct the transcontinental railroad and did manual labor like mining and agricultural work.

**Japanese Immigration**
	Came to Hawaii in the 1880s to participate in the sugar industry, and to California. 
	Their government strongly negotiated with the US government to protect their immigrants. 

**Korean and Vietnamese Immigration**
	Korean immigration mostly took place during the late 1900s. 
	Vietnamese immigration occurred post 1975, after the fall of Saigon and the establishment of restrictive communist policies in Vietnam. They sought asylum from harsh conditions in their homeland. 

**Intergroup Relations**
	**Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882**
		Abruptly ended Chinese immigration. 
		White workers blamed Chinese migrants for taking jobs. 
	**California Alien Land Law of 1913**
		Prohibited immigrants from owning land. 
	Japanese interment camps also happened during the 40s. 

**Model Minority**
	Applied to a minority group that is seen as reaching significant educational, professional, and socioeconomic levels without challenging the existing establishment.

**Asian Americans**
	Seen as the model minority in the US. This puts heavy expectations on Asians to be smart and productive. 


#### White Americans
**Why They Came**
	White ethnic Europeans formed the second and third great waves of immigration form the early 1800s - mid 1900s. 

**1820s Germans and Irish**
	Germans came for both economic opportunity and to escape political unrest and military conscription. 
	Irish people weren't as well off, especially after the Irish Potato Famine of 1845. Settled mainly on the East Coast, where they faced significant discrimination. 

**19th and 20th Century Eastern Europeans**
	Southern Italians, Russians, Polish, Bulgarians, and Austrians started arriving around the same time. 
	Forced into a hardscrabble existence in their native lands which drove them to seek better opportunities in the US. 
	Also included Jewish people escaping massacres in Eastern Europe and the Pale of Settlement. 

**Intergroup Relations**
	German immigrants weren't victimized with the exception being around WW1 and WW2. 
	Irish people were working poor class. 
	Southern and Eastern Europeans faced intense discrimination. Italians were segregated to slums in NE cities. 

**Current Status**
	German Americans are the largest group among White ethnic Americans in the country. 
	There are more Irish Americans than there are Irish in Ireland. 
	Italian Americans are almost completely culturally assimilated. 
	Jewish people formed tight-knit communities.


#### Hispanic Americans
**Hispanic and Latino**
	Refers to Cuban, Mexican, Puerto Rican, South / Central American, or other Spanish cultures. 

**How and Why They Came**
	Mexican migration to the US started in the 1900s in response to the need for inexpensive agricultural labor. 
	They'd work for a few years then return to Mexico. 
	Cuban Americans started after Fidel Castro came to power in 1959 and reached its crest with the Mariel boatlift in 1980. They moved generally to the Miami area. 

**Intergroup Relations**
	**Operation Wetback in 1954**
		Deported thousands of illegal Mexican workers. 
	**Douglas Massey**
		Suggests that although the average standard of living than in Mexico may be lower in the US, it's not so low as to make permanent migration the goal of most Mexicans. 

**Current Status**
	In recent years, triple the Mexican immigrants may have entered the US illegally as those who arrived legally. 
	Cuban Americans are often sees as a model minority group within the larger Hispanic group.
