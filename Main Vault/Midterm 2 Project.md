# Concept 1: George Herbert Mead’s Theory of Socialization

Taken from chapter 5, section 1. “Through socialization we learn to put ourselves in someone else's shoes and look at the world through their perspective. This assists us in becoming self-aware, as we look at ourselves from the perspective of the "other."”

Mead essentially argued that we aren’t born with the ability to see ourselves through others’ eyes. He believed that as we develop, we learn to imitate other people’s roles, to take on multiple roles at once, and then eventually to understand the common behavioral expectations of general society. Once we understand the generalized other, we can see through their eyes and develop self-awareness.

This is tangentially related to Charles’ Cooley’s theory of the Looking Glass Self. Cooley believed that our sense of self is based on how we think we look to others, and upon other people’s reactions. If Mead’s theory was a construction worker building a person’s self-image, Cooley’s theory would be his hammer. I think Cooley proposed the process in which the self-awareness development based on Mead’s generalized other occurs. I figured there was enough overlap to include here.

# Concept 2: Erving Goffman’s Theory of Dramaturgy

Taken from chapter 4, section 3. “Sociologist Erving Goffman presented the idea that a person is like an actor on a stage... Each situation is a new scene, and individuals perform different roles depending on who is present.”

Goffman believed that depending on the context, we subconsciously assume multiple invisible roles and alter our behaviors around them. Roles can conflict, be learned, be chosen, be given to us, and cause physical/mental strain if too much is demanded of them. You can have multiple roles simultaneously, like “man” and “student,” and they collectively form your behavior.

# Concept 3: The Davis-Moore Thesis

Taken from chapter 9, section 4. “The greater the functional importance of a social role, the greater must be the reward. The theory posits that social stratification represents the inherently unequal value of different work.”

In a sense, Davis and Moore posited that social classes exist to promote those doing work important to society. The valued a given task / social role is, the more it is promoted by society, and consequently, the higher class they are. It’s been thoroughly criticized for not accounting for several obvious exceptions, like professional athletes, old money, and people who amassed power by accident.

# How They Connect
