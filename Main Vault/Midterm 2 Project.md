# Concept 1: George Herbert Mead’s Theory of Socialization

Taken from chapter 5, section 1. “Through socialization we learn to put ourselves in someone else's shoes and look at the world through their perspective. This assists us in becoming self-aware, as we look at ourselves from the perspective of the "other."” Broadly, it's about learning roles and gaining self-awareness.

Mead essentially argued that we aren’t born with the ability to see ourselves through others’ eyes. He believed that as we develop, 
1. We learn to imitate other people’s roles
2. We learn to take on multiple roles at once and to understand how multiple roles function together
3. We learn the common behavioral expectations of general society; we understand the social role of the generalized other. 

Essentially, he believed that we develop understanding of what social roles are and what they're for as we mature, and that that development concludes with us understanding the general behavior of someone in society. Once we understand how others think, we can see ourselves from their perspective and develop self awareness.

This is tangentially related to Charles’ Cooley’s theory of the Looking Glass Self. Cooley believed that our sense of self is based on how we think we look to others, and upon other people’s reactions. If Mead’s theory was a construction worker building a person’s self-image, Cooley’s theory would be his hammer; I think Cooley proposed the process in which the self-awareness development based on Mead’s generalized other occurs. I figured there was enough overlap to mention this here.

# Concept 2: Erving Goffman’s Theory of Dramaturgy

Taken from chapter 4, section 3. “Sociologist Erving Goffman presented the idea that a person is like an actor on a stage... Each situation is a new scene, and individuals perform different roles depending on who is present.” Broadly, it's about switching roles and impression management.

Goffman believed that depending on the context, we subconsciously assume multiple invisible roles and alter our behaviors around them. Roles can conflict, be learned, be chosen, be given to us, and cause physical/mental strain if too much is demanded of them. You can have multiple roles simultaneously, like “man” and “student,” and they collectively form your behavior. 

It's interesting from an interactionists perspective because it gives an interesting framework in which to kind of predict social interactions based on context. You couldn't do it perfectly, but if you had a deep understanding of the values, behaviors, and desired perceptions of two interacting roles, you could make reasonable predictions on how things will play out.


# Concept 3: The Davis-Moore Thesis

Taken from chapter 9, section 4. “The greater the functional importance of a social role, the greater must be the reward. The theory posits that social stratification represents the inherently unequal value of different work.” Broadly, it's about society rewards different societal roles.

In a sense, Davis and Moore posited that social classes exist to promote those doing work important to society. The valued a given task / social role is, the more it is promoted by society, and consequently, the higher class they are. 

It’s been thoroughly criticized for not accounting for several obvious exceptions. For example, professional athletes, people who come from money, and people who amass wealth on accident, don't provide much value to society, but are promoted to the upper class. 


# How They Connect

Mead studied how we learn and take on roles, Goffman studied how we manage roles to guide how we’re perceived, and Davis and Moore studied how different societal roles are valued more than others. They all relate to different roles people in society take throughout their lives, and why they matter. 


# Why They Matter

**Mead and Goffman** 
These two theories work well together to analyze both how primitive roles in children are managed as they're learned, and how ones view of themselves changes when exposed to different roles.

**Mead and Davis-Moore**
These two theories could be used to study how people who were exposed to either valued or unvalued societal roles develop self-awareness. I think it could be very interesting to analyze changes in someone's self worth if they develop an understanding of the generalized other based on valued societal roles they can't measure up to.

**Goffman and Davis-Moore**
I think these two theories could be used to look at societal pressures, role conflicts, and role strain across different layers of social stratification. There are some interesting questions that could be asked about the meanings and interactions of roles in different classes. 

**Perspectives**
Using all three, 
- A functionalist could look at why social roles matter, and what function they actually provide to society. 
- An interactionist could look at how roles influence behavior, socialization, and the transmission of culture. 
- A conflict theorist could look at how roles perpetuate social stratification and power imbalances.
